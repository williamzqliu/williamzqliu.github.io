The brief was to redesign an existing piece of information design. I chose an infographic that argued against driving under the influence, and spent three weeks rebuilding both its argument and its data presentation.

The original is *INCOME/OUTCOME*, published by the Marketplace Wealth & Poverty desk. It is reproduced here for the purpose of critique; all rights remain with its publisher.

## 01 The problem

The original compares three income brackets across car ownership, commuting, homeownership, marriage and drinking, then closes on a line about drunk driving. Reading it carefully, four separate problems surface, and they are not the same problem.

**The logical connection to DUI is missing.** Nearly all of the surface area goes to income-based lifestyle differences. None of it establishes a link to DUI risk or DUI consequences. The closing line about the legal limit arrives from nowhere.

**Much of the data is irrelevant.** Car ownership and drinking habits have an indirect connection. Marriage counts, pet ownership, bird and gerbil ownership and homeownership have none.

**The encodings mislead.** The half-filled car illustrations cannot be read as proportions, and the arrows cross each other in a way that leaves no reliable reading order.

**The visual hierarchy points at the wrong subject.** The largest, most saturated elements are cars and manufacturer logos. A reader scanning it would reasonably conclude the subject is car preference.

Separating these mattered more than the individual findings. A single verdict of "this is cluttered" would have licensed a cosmetic fix. Four distinct failures pointed at four distinct decisions, and one of them, the broken argument, could not be solved by any amount of layout work.

## 02 Approach

The original intent was worth keeping: use everyday life and spending to reach DUI, and end on a case against it. What needed replacing was the reasoning in between.

I wrote out three strategies before drawing anything.

**Income and DUI probability.** If DUI rates were similar across income brackets, that would establish DUI as everyone's problem rather than a particular group's. Rejected: the causal link is weak and hard to support with available data.

**DUI's impact on both families.** Compare households that cause DUI incidents with households harmed by them. Rejected: no comparable data on both sides exists.

**DUI costs against household expenses.** Drop income brackets entirely. Convert national losses into a per-household figure and set it beside ordinary monthly expenses. Chosen: both sides of that comparison are published by government agencies and research institutions, so the argument can be checked by the reader.

The strategies were eliminated on data availability, not on which one felt strongest. Two arguments I found more interesting than the one I built could not be sourced, and that settled it.

## 03 What I built

A print infographic in four movements.

**Establish the scale.** $296 billion in annual losses, broken into its nine components: lost productivity, workplace costs, insurance administration, congestion impacts, quality-of-life valuations, medical costs, emergency medical services, property damage, and legal and court costs. The breakdown is there for credibility, not for reading closely.

**Bring it down to one household.** $296,000,000,000 divided by 125,736,353 households is $2,354. The division is shown as a labelled equation rather than stated as a result, because the reader needs to see that nothing was smuggled in.

**Convert it into things people buy.** Average monthly rent of $1,983, so 1.19 months. A car loan of $1,884, so 1.25 months. A moderate food plan of $1,190, so 1.98 months. Family health coverage of $1,997, so 1.18 months. One semester of tuition at a four-year public institution of $4,917, so 0.48 semesters. Against the poverty line the comparison runs the other way: $2,354 exceeds the monthly income of a household of four ($2,313) and more than doubles the monthly income of one person ($1,133).

**End where money stops working as a unit.** In 2022, 1,129 children aged fourteen and under were killed in motor vehicle crashes in the United States. 283 of them, one in four, died in alcohol-impaired crashes. The education comparison says this money could have gone to a child's schooling. The 283 says some children never got as far as needing tuition. Those two sentences are deliberately not combined, because combining them converts a life into a dollar figure.

## 04 Key decisions

**Dropping from national scale to a single household is the whole redesign.** $296 billion is a number no one can feel. $2,354 is a month of rent. The arithmetic is trivial; choosing to perform it is the design decision.

**Ratios over raw amounts.** "1.98 months of groceries" lands where "$1,190" does not, because readers have an intuition for months and none for other households' grocery bills.

**The poverty-line comparison comes first and uses a household-size ladder.** It is the only place in the piece where the loss figure is larger than an income figure, so it runs before the median-income comparisons. The ladder from one person to four gives the reader a row to locate themselves on.

**Colour was reassigned to carry categories, not emphasis.** The first version leaned on red and yellow together. Yellow is loud, its shades are hard to separate, and I had not accounted for the difference between screen and print colour. The final system gives each colour one job: red for losses and key figures, yellow for households and expenditures, dark blue for demographic and social data.

**Three pieces of critique changed the layout.** Sheila Pontis pushed for less colour. Gab questioned how elements sat inside the icons. Chaitanya asked for tighter margins and larger key figures. The last one mattered most: in the first version, $2,354 and 283, the two numbers the whole argument rests on, did not stand out from their surroundings.

<summary>Data notes and where the argument is soft</summary>

**$296 billion is a comprehensive cost figure, not cash any household spent.** The source separates tangible losses ($58 billion, about $461 per household) from a comprehensive total that includes quality-of-life valuations ($296 billion, about $2,354 per household). I used the comprehensive figure because it is the official total, but strictly speaking the claim "this could have paid rent" holds only for the tangible portion. The piece does not say this and it should.

**The years do not match.** The loss figure is for 2019; the household count is 2022. The magnitude of the result survives, the precision does not.

**Dividing by all households assumes an even distribution that does not exist.** This is a rhetorical device for making a number legible, not a statistical claim about who bears the cost.

**Sources.** NHTSA and HHS for the loss figures. U.S. Census for household counts and income distribution. USDA Food and Nutrition Service for food plan costs. NCES for tuition. KFF for health insurance. Experian for auto loans. An April 2024 rent report for rental costs.

## 05 Outcome

A finished print piece, an initial version and a final version that can be read side by side, and a documented path from three written strategies through concept notes and sketches to delivery.

What it does not have: any evaluation. No reader testing, no exhibition outside the course, no published outcome. The critique it received was formative and internal. I would rather say that plainly than describe a studio review as validation.

## 06 Reflection

The useful thing I learned here was not about layout. It was that a redesign's hardest constraint is data availability. Two of my three strategies were more interesting than the one I built, and both died because the numbers to support them do not exist in public form. Recognising that early cost a day of writing and saved three weeks of building an argument I could not defend.

The part I would fix now is the quality-of-life caveat. The piece treats $2,354 as money that could have been spent on rent, and roughly a fifth of it is. Stating the tangible figure alongside the comprehensive one would have cost a single line and made the argument harder to attack.
