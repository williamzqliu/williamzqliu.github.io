Three reasons this does not get a case study:

1. Third-party material. Three separate kinds, at different levels of risk.
   The left column reproduces the complete lyrics of Katy Perry's "Witness",
   which is verbatim reproduction of a whole copyrighted work and has no
   parody defence. The Spotify wordmark, the Wrapped identity system and the
   player chrome are trademark use, defensible as student parody but needing
   an explicit statement of no affiliation. The artist photograph beside "My
   Top Song" is third-party copyright as well. Handling all three costs more
   than the piece returns, and handling them removes what makes it striking.

2. The eight Gestalt principles are the single most redrawn subject in
   information design. That section is competently made and carries no
   differentiation.

3. Most of the visual framework is borrowed. The palette, the layout and the
   components are Spotify's. That was a smart choice for a course assignment
   and a liability in a portfolio, where the honest answer to "which parts are
   yours" is the structure and the diagrams, not the visual system.

Where it is still useful: it is the only evidence in the archive for grounding
in visual perception theory. Asked in an interview about preattentive
processing, the what and where pathways, saccades and visual search, or
Gestalt, this is the artifact that shows the material was studied rather than
skimmed. Mention it; do not present it as design work.

The one design decision worth stating out loud: a mind map has no reading
order, and the assignment asked for a mind map. The song supplied a linear
sequence, and that sequence runs in the same direction as perception itself,
from feature detection through comparison and search to recognition and
finally pattern. The structure came from the temporal shape of the content,
not from a visual idea.
