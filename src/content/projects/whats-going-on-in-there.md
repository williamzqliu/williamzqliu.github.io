## What it is

An interactive exhibit about what caffeine and sleep do to your brain, built for
incoming college students. A visitor draws a question card, answers it, picks up a
rubber band in the colour matching their answer, and loops it around the pins of the
two brain regions the card names. Twelve cards later, they step back and look at what
they have built.

Whichever colour dominates the board is their result, and there is a card for each
colour explaining what that pattern means and what to do about it.

## My part, and what wasn't mine

Four of us built this over seven weeks: Carmen on organisation and creative
leadership, Ravi on the interactive mechanics, Yi-Ting on visual execution, and me on
concepts, prototyping, key visuals, user testing, and documentation.

The pinboard-and-rubber-band mechanic came from Ravi. I helped refine it, and it would
be dishonest to present the central idea of the project as mine. What I did contribute
runs through everything below: the concept exploration that got us to a physical
mechanic at all, the 2D and 3D prototypes we tested with, the visual work on the
panels and cards, running the testing sessions, and keeping a record of why each
version changed.

Saying that plainly is the point. On a four-person project the interesting question in
an interview is not what the team made, it is what you were responsible for and how
you know.

## Narrowing the topic

We started far too wide: food chemistry, neuroscience, how the body works. The middle
stage was closer, framed as how sleep, food, water and exercise affect the brain and
how you keep balanced habits in college. Still too broad to design anything specific.

What decided it was the audience rather than the subject. Incoming freshmen are
precisely the group with irregular sleep and high caffeine intake, and precisely the
group who have not yet connected the two to how their own thinking feels. Once the
audience was fixed, caffeine and sleep was the obvious cut and everything else fell
away.

## Why a physical mechanic

Concept exploration went through a board game, a freshman orientation activity, a
brochure, a quiz, an escape room, and a card bingo. Most of them communicate
information. The pinboard does something else: the visitor's own habits become a
physical object in front of them.

That is the mechanism worth explaining. Each answer maps to a colour, A through D as
red, yellow, green, blue, and each card names two brain regions. The visitor connects
those two regions with a band in their answer's colour. After twelve cards the board
carries a distribution of colour that nobody designed and nobody typed in.

**The visitor builds their own data visualisation.** Not a form, not a quiz result
computed for them. They can see the answer taking shape while they are still answering,
and the moment of reading it is the moment they step back.

The last step adds a collective layer: a sticker on a shared board, so a visitor sees
their pattern against everyone else's. Individual result and group distribution in the
same frame, which cost nothing and changed what the exhibit was about.

## Testing with three different groups

We tested with our own cohort, with neuroscience students, and with incoming freshmen,
and the reason for three groups is that they answered three different questions.

The neuroscience students were not there for usability. They were there for scientific
accuracy, checking that the brain regions were labelled correctly and that the causal
claims on the cards were defensible. An exhibit that teaches something wrong is worse
than no exhibit.

The freshmen tested comprehension: could someone who had never seen it work out what
to do without being told.

Our own cohort tested visual hierarchy and readability.

Choosing the right participants for each question, rather than running the same session
three times, is the part of this I would carry into any research plan.

## Iteration, including the version we killed

The panels went through five versions, the question cards four, the result cards three.
All of it is documented.

The result cards are the clearest progression: version one was a solid paragraph,
version two split into Results, Facts and Tips, and the final version added colour
identification and an illustration. Each step removed words. In an exhibit nobody
stands and reads a paragraph.

The panel version worth mentioning is the one we did not use. Version four was a comic
treatment, a talking brain character with speech bubbles, visibly more fun than what
shipped. It was also unfinished, and the team judged it as too much to execute properly
in the time left. We chose the plainer version and finished it.

I liked version four. Recording it as a decision rather than quietly dropping it is the
honest version of what happened, and getting something finished and in front of real
visitors turned out to matter more than the treatment.

## What came back

The exhibit ran with real visitors. The photographs show people drawing cards, looping
bands, placing stickers, and there is a row of sticky notes visitors left unprompted:
*I lied*, *Sorry my brain :(*, *I've got a great brain*, *stay healthy*.

Those notes are the most useful outcome. Nobody asked for them, and they are evidence
that visitors got as far as reflecting on their own behaviour rather than just reading
a wall.

What we do not have is any measurement: no visitor count, no time-on-exhibit, no
distribution of answers, even though the board itself was accumulating exactly that
data in the form of coloured bands. In retrospect the mechanic generated a dataset and
we photographed it instead of recording it, which is the thing I would fix first.
